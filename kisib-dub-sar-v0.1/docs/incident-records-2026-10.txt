INC-2026-10-A  ccTLD registry hijacks (.gh .sl .as)
======================================================================
Class: DNS / registry hijack. Domain control was stolen; no CA key was lost.
What happened: attackers who compromised the registries for .gh (Ghana),
  .sl (Sierra Leone) and .as (American Samoa) changed authoritative DNS
  records and obtained unauthorized HTTPS certificates for Google domains
  and domains of other unnamed organizations. Google's systems were not
  compromised.
Disclosed: 2026-10-06 (Google security blog). Google learned of it the
  week before.
Issuing CAs: not named in Google's post. The Oct 23 chat named Let's Encrypt
  and ZeroSSL from press reports [to test]. Google says it has no reason to
  believe the CAs did anything wrong.
Certificate hashes: NOT published. The hash list stays empty. A MISS against
  this incident never means safe.
Chrome response: blocked the Google-property certificates through CRLSets,
  had the CAs revoke them, and used CT logs to find and block certificates
  for other affected organizations.
Google recommendations: CT monitoring across the whole domain portfolio;
  restrictive CAA records (RFC 8657 account/method binding); do not rely on
  browser-side intervention for non-Chrome users.

What this means for kisib:
- A country, owner or root block does NOT stop this class. The rogue certs
  chain to ordinary, legitimately trusted public CAs.
- Detection needs CT issuance data (S5 "issued") compared with the encountered
  certificate (S5 "encountered", CAPI2) and the flag "issuing CA changed for
  this domain".
- The ccTLD is registry geography only. It is never joined to a certificate
  C= or to the CA owner's country (three country notions stay separate).

INC-2026-10-B  eMudhra (emSign) intermediate CA key compromise
======================================================================
Class: CA private-key compromise. Anyone holding the keys could sign
  certificates trusted for any site by clients that still trusted the chain.
Cause (as published): the intermediate private keys were generated with weak
  entropy and are recoverable from the certificates. Possible since the
  certificates were created in April 2025.
Proof: the reporter signed the message "We have a problem here - Wayne" with
  each key (proof.zip; verify with openssl dgst -sha384 -verify).
Timeline (UTC unless stated):
  2026-10-17 18:05  Chrome Root Program receives the report.
  2026-10-17 19:45  Chrome ships a CRLSet blocking the intermediates by SPKI hash.
  2026-10-17        Firefox blocks.
  2026-10-18        eMudhra revokes its 8 originals (reason ca_compromise).
  2026-10-19        Sectigo revokes the 8 cross-signs. Disclosure to downstream
                    trust stores. Android/Googlebooks via Play Services.
  2026-10-20        Microsoft adds the 16 certificates to the Windows disallowed
                    certificate list (Fleet guide). oss-security post.
  2026-10-22        Apple blocks (per reporter page; the Oct 23 chat said Oct 19
                    [to test]).
  2026-10-27 (PDT)  Chrome removes emSign ECC Root CA - G3 from the Chrome Root
                    Store (planned).
CT: no TLS certificates chaining to the affected intermediates were logged
  before the block. CT logs now reject them.

The 8 keys exist as 16 certificates: each eMudhra-issued original has a
Sectigo cross-sign (issuer AAA Certificate Services, Comodo CA Limited, C=GB)
with the SAME subject and SAME SPKI. Blocking by certificate hash needs all 16;
blocking by SPKI needs 8.

Country notions on one chain (the stacked-tag case):
  subject C=  NL (eMudhra BV) or US (eMudhra Inc)
  issuer C=   IN (emSign root) or GB (AAA Certificate Services cross-sign)
  CA owner    eMudhra Technologies Limited, India
  A join on the certificate's own C= tags these NL or US, not IN. The India
  tag comes from the issuer/owner notion. Keep the notions separate and let
  the tags stack.

Algorithms: all keys EC secp384r1. Originals signed ecdsa-with-SHA384;
cross-signs signed sha256WithRSAEncryption.

Windows (Microsoft term: untrusted / Disallowed certificate trust list):
- Microsoft's protection is the disallowed certificate list Windows downloads
  automatically (disallowedcertstl.cab, Disallowed store). [documented
  mechanism; the Oct 20 addition per Fleet, to test]
- Check sync: certutil -verifyctl Disallowed | findstr /i "lastsynctime"
  Fleet's guide: passes when last sync is on or after 2026-10-21 UTC.
- Policy that can stop it: "Untrusted CTL Automatic Update"
  (EnableDisallowedCertAutoUpdate) and "Auto Root Update"
  (DisableRootAutoUpdate). A PC with automatic update turned off never gets
  Microsoft's block.
  https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-cs/configure-trusted-roots-disallowed-certificates

What this means for kisib:
- This class IS answered by a block list. It is the first incident with
  published hashes, so it is the first real test row for the S5 incident CSV
  and the S2 block dry run.
- Block on SPKI SHA-256, not only the certificate SHA-256 (Chrome's method).
  One SPKI row covers the original and its cross-sign.
- Screen 1 already shows the evidence needed to see this on a PC: the
  Disallowed system store, its physical stores and counts, SHA-1/SHA-256, and
  SPKI SHA-256 in Details.
- Chrome and Firefox decide with their own root stores and CRLSets/OneCRL.
  Windows' Disallowed store is not proof any browser blocked it, and a Chrome
  block is not proof Windows did. Each stays a separate observed state.
